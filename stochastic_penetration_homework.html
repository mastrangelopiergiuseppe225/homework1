1. Model and assumptions

We model the aggregate penetration score after n steps as a sum of independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) random increments \(X_1,\ldots,X_n\). A trajectory is one simulated realization of these increments.

Bernoulli model

Xᵢ ∈ {0, 1};   P(Xᵢ = 1) = p

A step either adds one successful penetration point (probability p) or adds zero. The expected increment is p, and its variance is p(1 − p).

E[Xᵢ] = p   ·   Var(Xᵢ) = p(1 − p)

Rademacher model

Xᵢ ∈ {−1, +1};   P(Xᵢ = ±1) = 1/2

Each step contributes either +1 or −1 with equal probability. Positive and negative increments balance on average, making this a symmetric, zero-mean model.

E[Xᵢ] = 0   ·   Var(Xᵢ) = 1
Interpretation of n and m. Here, n is the number of accumulated steps/systems represented in the sum. m is the number of attackers. The prompt does not specify how multiple attackers alter the distribution of each increment, so the simulation treats m as contextual information rather than silently changing p or the increment law. To model attacker interactions, one would need an explicit rule connecting m to success probabilities, dependence, or the number of attempts.

2. Quantities of interest

Total score

Sₙ = Σ Xᵢ

The cumulative score. Its typical magnitude depends on both drift and random fluctuations.

Average score

Sₙ / n

The score per step. This removes growth proportional to the number of steps.

Normalized total

Sₙ / √n

The fluctuation scale used by the Central Limit Theorem when increments have finite, nonzero variance.

4. Law of Large Numbers (LLN)

If the increments are i.i.d. with finite mean μ, the Strong Law of Large Numbers implies that the sample average converges to μ almost surely; in particular, it converges in probability:

Sₙ/n → μ in probability as n → ∞

For the Bernoulli model, μ = p, so the average fraction of successful steps approaches p. For the Rademacher model, μ = 0, so the average net score approaches zero. In the Rademacher case, typical total fluctuations are still of order √n, but dividing by n makes them shrink.

Var(Sₙ/n) = σ²/n → 0

Chebyshev's inequality makes the probability statement explicit: for every ε > 0, P(|Sₙ/n − μ| ≥ ε) ≤ σ²/(nε²), which tends to zero.

5. Why the normalizations behave differently

Total score Sₙ

Its variance grows linearly with n. In the Bernoulli model, the positive mean also makes the total typically grow near np. In the symmetric Rademacher model, the mean stays at zero while the standard deviation grows as √n.

Var(Sₙ) = nσ²

Average score Sₙ/n

Its variance decreases like 1/n, so repeated trajectories become increasingly concentrated around the mean μ. This is the LLN scaling.

Var(Sₙ/n) = σ²/n

Normalized total Sₙ/√n

Its variance remains constant. For Rademacher increments it is exactly 1, so this scaling preserves the random fluctuations instead of making them vanish.

Var(Sₙ/√n) = σ²

Important Bernoulli distinction

Since Bernoulli increments have positive mean p (when p > 0), Sₙ/√n has mean p√n, which grows. A centered CLT statement is therefore needed:

(Sₙ − np) / √(np(1 − p)) ⇒ N(0,1)

For fixed 0 < p < 1. This differs from the uncentered Rademacher normalization.

6. Central Limit Theorem (CLT): Rademacher case

The Rademacher increments have mean μ = 0 and variance σ² = 1. The classical CLT states that for i.i.d. increments with finite variance:

(Sₙ − nμ) / (σ√n) ⇒ N(0,1)

Substituting μ = 0 and σ = 1 gives the required result directly:

Sₙ / √n ⇒ N(0,1)

The symbol ⇒ means convergence in distribution: as n increases, the distribution of the normalized total approaches the standard normal distribution. Individual values do not have to converge to a fixed number. The histogram becomes more bell-shaped, while the normalized variance stays at 1.

For finite n, Sₙ takes only integer values with the same parity as n, so the distribution is discrete. The normal curve is a limiting approximation, not an exact finite-sample distribution.

7. Interpretation: systems and attackers

Think of each increment as the net contribution of one step in a penetration process. Under the Bernoulli model, each step contributes either zero or one success. Under the Rademacher model, each step contributes +1 or −1, representing a symmetric gain/loss or success/failure balance.

  • Increasing n: the total accumulates more increments; its variance grows as n. The average becomes more stable because its variance shrinks as 1/n.
  • Rademacher normalization: dividing by √n keeps fluctuations on an order-one scale, revealing the normal shape predicted by the CLT.
  • Increasing m: more attackers may plausibly mean more attempts or a higher aggregate penetration probability, but that conclusion requires an additional model. For example, if each of m independent attackers independently succeeds with probability q in a system, the probability of at least one success would be pₘ = 1 − (1 − q)ᵐ. That is an optional extension, not an assumption in the stated homework.
  • Independence caveat: real attackers may coordinate, share vulnerabilities, or compete for resources. Such effects can violate the i.i.d. assumption and change both the variance and limiting behavior.

8. Conclusions

The simulations illustrate three complementary views of the same sum. The raw total shows accumulation; the average isolates the long-run mean and concentrates around it by the LLN; and the square-root normalization retains fluctuations, which converge to a standard normal law in the zero-mean Rademacher model by the CLT. The number of systems/steps n has a precise mathematical role. The number of attackers m matters only after the model specifies how attackers affect increments or attempts.
